Study design is the plan for turning a question into evidence. It defines the target population,
the quantities to estimate, the observations or interventions needed, and the comparisons that
will support the answer. It also states which claims the resulting evidence cannot support.

A design should identify the units being studied, how they enter the study, what treatment or
exposure each unit receives, which outcomes are measured, and when measurement occurs. The
analysis belongs in the same plan. A precise model cannot recover a comparison that the data never
made possible.

## Start with the question and the target

Descriptive, predictive, and causal questions need different evidence. Estimating the prevalence
of a condition asks how common it is in a defined population. Predicting an outcome asks how well
available information forecasts it in the setting where the prediction will be used. Estimating a
causal effect asks how an outcome would differ under alternative actions or exposures.

The target must be concrete enough to design around. "Does the treatment work?" leaves out the
population, treatment versions, comparison, outcome, and period. Those choices define the
estimand, the quantity the study is trying to estimate. Changing one can change the answer without
any calculation being wrong.

[Hernán and Robins](https://miguelhernan.org/whatifbook) use a hypothetical target trial to make
these choices explicit for causal questions. An observational study can then try to emulate that
trial. Its causal interpretation depends on assumptions about confounding, selection, measurement,
and the analysis. Observational data can support causal claims when those assumptions are credible.
The data alone cannot establish them.

## Assignment and sampling solve different problems

[Randomisation](/ideas/randomization) assigns treatments or conditions within a study. A known
assignment procedure can make treatment groups comparable and support inference based on the
design. It primarily strengthens claims about differences caused by the assigned treatment.

Sampling determines which units enter the study. A probability sample can support estimates for a
target population when the sampling frame, participation, measurement, and analysis match that
goal. [Sampling bias](/ideas/sampling-bias) arises when the inclusion process systematically
distorts the estimate.

Random treatment assignment does not make the participants representative of a wider population.
Random sampling does not assign treatments or remove confounding between an observed exposure and
an outcome. A study can use either process, both, or neither. The claims must follow the processes
it actually used.

## Validity depends on the whole design

A successful random assignment can still sit inside a weak study. Participants may not follow
their assignment, outcomes may be measured differently between groups, or attrition may depend on
both treatment and outcome. A precise estimate for the enrolled sample may not apply to the target
population.

Design choices should cover:

* recruitment, eligibility, and the sampling frame;
* treatment, exposure, comparison, and assignment;
* outcome definitions, measurement procedures, and follow-up;
* missing data, non-adherence, and departures from the plan;
* an analysis tied to the design and the estimand;
* ethical constraints, including who bears risks and who can benefit.

[Data provenance](/ideas/data-provenance) can record how these choices produced the analysed data.
[Feature engineering](/ideas/feature-engineering) can change what a model learns from those data.
It cannot repair an unidentified target or a comparison the design did not create.

The originating essay, ["Effective and Ethical Data Science Study
Design"](/blog/2023-02-24-effective-ethical-data-science-study-design), argues that data collection
and modelling decisions carry ethical consequences. That is a useful prompt for design review.
The author presents the broader philosophical claims as an argument. They are separate from
statistical definitions.

## Questions it prompts

* What exact quantity should the study estimate, for which population and period?
* Which comparison makes that quantity identifiable?
* How do units enter the study, receive an exposure or treatment, and leave the analysis?
* Which assumptions connect the observed data to the intended claim?
* Which design failures could the planned analysis detect, and which would remain hidden?
