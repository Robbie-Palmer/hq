Sampling bias is systematic error caused by the process that produces the observed sample. It
matters when inclusion, participation, retention, or exclusion is related to the quantity being
estimated. The result can then differ from the answer for the target population even when the
measurements and calculations within the sample are correct.

The target population, sampling frame, recruited sample, and analysed sample are separate things.
A sampling frame may omit eligible people. Some selected people may not participate. Some
participants may leave before follow-up or lack data required by the analysis. Each step can change
who contributes to the estimate.

## Bias depends on the question

A sample does not need to match its target population on every characteristic. The relevant issue
is whether the selection process changes the estimate for the question being asked. An age
imbalance matters for estimating a population mean when age predicts the outcome. It may not matter
for another quantity that does not vary with age.

[Lesko and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10193086/) distinguish a sample
that supports the same numerical estimate as its target population from one whose scientific
interpretation can generalise despite different population characteristics. Calling either sample
"representative" without naming the target and the intended inference hides that distinction.

Common routes to sampling bias include:

* undercoverage, when the sampling frame omits part of the target population;
* convenience or self-selection, when availability or willingness affects inclusion;
* non-response, when selected units do not provide the requested observations;
* attrition, when remaining in follow-up depends on relevant characteristics or outcomes;
* analytic exclusions, when missing values or eligibility rules change the analysed sample.

These mechanisms can operate together. A probability sample can become biased through non-response
or attrition. A non-probability sample may still yield an unbiased estimate. Connecting that
estimate to the target population requires assumptions or external information about inclusion.

## Bias is not sampling error

Random sampling error is variation between samples produced by the same chance procedure. Under a
valid probability design, estimates may land above or below the population value by chance while
remaining unbiased across repeated samples. Increasing the sample size reduces this variation
under common probability designs.

Sampling bias shifts the expected estimate away from its target, although the direction may be
unknown. Collecting more observations through the same biased inclusion process can make the
result more precise without making it more accurate. The confidence interval can narrow around the
wrong value.

Weighting, stratification, standardisation, and model-based adjustment can reduce bias when the
required variables are measured and the assumptions are credible. They cannot recover an omitted
group whose relevant differences are unknown. In an open empirical study, [Sturgis and
colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9638233/) found that routine demographic
adjustment sometimes reduced discrepancies between convenience and representative samples and
sometimes increased them. Adjustment needs a theory of the selection process, not a default list
of covariates.

## Sampling and assignment have different targets

[Randomisation](/ideas/randomization) assigns treatments within the recruited study. It can protect
a treatment comparison from confounding without making the participants representative of a wider
population. Random sampling selects units from a population but does not assign their exposure.
[Study design](/ideas/study-design) must say which inference each process supports.

The causal-inference term "selection bias" is broader than sampling bias. It also covers bias
introduced by conditioning on variables affected by an exposure and an outcome, even when the
original recruitment process was sound. The terms overlap when selection into the observed or
analysed sample causes the problem, but they are not synonyms in every use.

The originating essay, ["Effective and Ethical Data Science Study
Design"](/blog/2023-02-24-effective-ethical-data-science-study-design), stresses that data
collection shapes the models trained on it. [Feature engineering](/ideas/feature-engineering) can
change how a model represents the sample. It cannot supply evidence about people or cases that the
selection process never observed. [Data provenance](/ideas/data-provenance) can at least record the
frames, exclusions, response patterns, and weighting steps behind the result.

## Questions it prompts

* What is the target population, and which list or process can reach it?
* How can inclusion, response, retention, or exclusion depend on the outcome or its predictors?
* Does "representative" refer to population characteristics, a numerical estimate, or an
  interpretation?
* Which assumptions make weighting or adjustment valid, and can the available data test them?
* Would collecting more data repeat the same selection process or change it?
