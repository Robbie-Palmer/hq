Delegated authority lets one principal authorize another actor to perform selected actions without
sharing the principal's complete identity or permissions. A useful grant names the actor, resources,
allowed actions, constraints, expiry, and the principal that approved it.

The resource remains authoritative. It must verify the actor's credential, the delegated grant, its
own policy, and the delegator's current permission. A grant can narrow existing authority. It cannot
create authority that the delegator does not have.

## Keep the actor visible

Delegation differs from impersonation. An impersonating actor appears to be the subject. A delegated
actor acts for the subject while retaining its own identity. That distinction supports audit,
actor-specific policy, and revocation.

[RFC 8693](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8693.html) makes this distinction in OAuth token
exchange. A subject token identifies the principal whose authority is involved. An actor token can
identify the party acting for that subject. The requested resource, audience, and scope constrain the
new token.

An audit record should therefore retain both identities. Recording only the user hides which service,
application, or agent performed the action. Recording only the actor loses the source of its
authority.

## Intersect permissions at the resource

The effective permission is the intersection of these checks:

* the delegator currently has permission on the resource;
* the grant permits the actor, action, and resource;
* time, audience, budget, network, and other conditions hold; and
* the resource's own policy permits the operation.

[AWS session policies](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/IAM/latest/UserGuide/id_credentials_temp_control-access_assumerole.html)
show this narrowing rule in practice. A session policy limits the permissions of an assumed role and
cannot grant permissions beyond the role's identity policy. GitHub App user access tokens follow a
similar intersection: an action needs both the app's permission and the user's permission, and the
token can reach only resources available to both.

Enforcement belongs at the service that owns the resource or at a gateway it trusts. A user interface
that hides an unavailable button is not an authorization check. Neither is a signed token if the
resource ignores its audience, scope, expiry, or actor.

## Make grants inspectable and revocable

A person approving a grant needs concrete terms: who will act, on which resource, with which actions,
for how long, and under what limits. Broad labels such as "repository access" hide the difference
between reading code, creating a branch, opening a pull request, and merging it.

Short-lived credentials reduce exposure but do not replace revocation. The system needs a way to end
one grant or actor without disabling the user's unrelated access. The resource must also recheck live
authority when removing a user's permission should stop an actor before its delegated credential
expires.

Revocation has a propagation boundary. A service that validates a token locally may accept it until
expiry unless it consults current state. A gateway can enforce revocation immediately for requests
that pass through it, but it cannot retract an operation that has already completed or control a
copied credential used through another path.

## Do not confuse authority with oversight

Authentication establishes an identity. It does not decide what that identity may do. Delegation
adds a source and scope for authority, while the resource still makes the authorization decision.

[Human in the loop](/ideas/human-in-the-loop) describes a person deciding before work continues.
[Human on the loop](/ideas/human-on-the-loop) describes supervision with the ability to intervene.
Either interaction model can approve or monitor a delegated grant, but neither defines the grant's
permissions or enforcement.

Delegation is not automatically transitive. An actor may delegate again only when the grant explicitly
permits it, and the next grant must remain within the authority received. A protocol can carry that
information, but it cannot make every receiving service interpret or enforce it correctly.

## Questions it prompts

* Which principal supplies the authority, and which actor uses it?
* What exact actions, resources, audiences, and time limits does the grant cover?
* Does the resource check both the delegator's live permission and the narrower grant?
* Can one actor or grant be revoked without ending unrelated access?
* Does the audit record distinguish delegation from impersonation?
