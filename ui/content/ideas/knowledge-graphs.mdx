A knowledge graph is a knowledge base organised as a network. Its parts might be notes, people,
places, concepts, sources, or decisions. Links record how those parts belong together: one note
develops another, a claim cites a source, a decision affects a system, or a person wrote a book.

This makes the graph useful while knowledge is still being formed. A new note does not need to fit
one folder or a finished taxonomy. It can be connected to what prompted it and to the ideas it
changes. Those connections provide routes back into the material and help later work recover its
context.

## What makes it knowledge

The word *knowledge* signals the purpose of the graph. It records what a person or community has
learned, asserted, interpreted, or decided. A network diagram, social graph, and route map are all
graphs, but they are not knowledge graphs merely because they contain nodes and edges.

The useful unit can be as large as a page or as small as a claim. A personal graph might connect a
research note to the book that prompted it and the decision it later informed. A public knowledge
base might record a claim about a person, the property being asserted, and a reference that
supports it. Both preserve knowledge through connections, although one is mainly read by a person
and the other is structured for machines too.

## Links let context accumulate

Folders place each item in a chosen location. Links let an item participate in several lines of
thought at once. Backlinks add the reverse view: when reading one note, a person can see the other
notes that depend on or mention it. [Obsidian describes internal links](https://obsidian.md/help/links)
as a way to create a network of knowledge, while
[Roam Research](https://roamresearch.com/) calls its approach "networked thought".

The graph visualisation is incidental. The retained context provides the benefit. A connection
made during research can still be followed months later; several small observations can
converge on the same concept; and an old decision can lead back to its evidence. Search retrieves
items that match a query. Connections also expose relevant material whose wording was different.

## A spectrum of explicitness

Knowledge graphs range from lightly structured notes to formal collections of statements.

At one end, a wiki or personal note system treats pages as nodes and hyperlinks as edges. The
meaning of a link may exist only in the surrounding prose. This is quick to write and easy for a
person to interpret, but a machine can do little more than follow the link or count it.

The next step is to distinguish kinds of content and relationships. A system might know that one
page is a source, another is a decision, and the edge between them means *cites* rather than merely
*links to*. It can then show backlinks by type, detect a decision with no supporting evidence, or
find everything affected by a change.

At the formal end, the graph represents individual claims using identified entities and named
predicates. "Ada wrote Program" becomes *Ada*, *wrote*, *Program*. Ontologies can define what
*wrote* means, and rules can derive further claims. The
[Stanford CS520 introduction](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs520/2020/notes/What_is_a_Knowledge_Graph.html)
focuses on this machine-readable form: a directed, labelled graph whose labels have well-defined
meanings.

These approaches occupy points on a continuum. A personal graph does not need RDF, global
identifiers, or inference to be useful. More formal semantics become worthwhile when software must
answer precise questions, combine claims at scale, or explain how it reached an answer.

## Wikipedia shows the distinction

Wikipedia is a connected body of knowledge written chiefly for people. Articles link to related
articles, cite sources, and grow through collective editing. The link from one article to another
usually does not state a machine-readable relationship.

[Wikidata](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Wikidata:Introduction), its structured companion, records
items and statements as property-value pairs. It can state that Douglas Adams was *educated at*
St John's College using identifiers for the author, property, and college. References and
qualifiers can say where a statement came from and when it applies. Wikipedia demonstrates the
human-readable, linked form; Wikidata demonstrates the more explicit knowledge graph.

## Structure should earn its cost

Typed links make certain questions possible, but they also make capture slower. Requiring every
new thought to use a settled ontology can interrupt the work the graph is meant to support. A
useful system can start with prose and ordinary links, then add types where a repeated question or
workflow needs them.

[Ontology engineering](/ideas/ontology-engineering) is the work of defining those shared concepts
and relationships. [Named entity recognition](/ideas/ner) can find mentions of people, places, and
other entities in prose. [Data provenance](/ideas/data-provenance) records where a claim came from
and how it changed. They can strengthen a knowledge graph, but none is a prerequisite for a
connected collection of knowledge.

The storage technology is also secondary. Relational tables, documents, RDF stores, and graph
databases can all implement a knowledge graph. What matters is whether the system preserves useful
connections between what is known, rather than whether the database happens to call them edges.

## Questions it prompts

* What knowledge is worth retaining beyond the task that produced it?
* Which connections would help someone recover the context of a note or claim later?
* Which relationships need explicit names, and which can remain links explained by prose?
* Where should sources, uncertainty, and disagreement be recorded?
* What repeated question would justify adding more structure?
