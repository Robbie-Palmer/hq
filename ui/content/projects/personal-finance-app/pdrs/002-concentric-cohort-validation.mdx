## Context

The product begins with a founder solving a personal spreadsheet problem. That
gives the work a concrete first user but creates a risk of fitting one unusual
financial setup. Broad acquisition would produce sign-ups before the core
workflow had proved useful and before the project could support a wide range of
households.

Visit frequency is also a poor success measure. A useful financial record may
need an update only a few times each month.

## Decision

Validate the product through expanding cohorts. The founder is the first cohort,
followed by the founder's partner, invited friends and colleagues, and finally a
general market. Move to the next cohort when the current one uses the product
without prompting and exposes needs that the next cohort can test.

Use committed data depth as the main product-fit signal. Relevant evidence
includes accounts tracked, history accumulated, recurring transfers, linked
accounts, and multi-entity relationships. Do not treat page views, daily visits,
or registrations as proof that the product has replaced a spreadsheet.

The founder deleting the source spreadsheet is the first acceptance signal. A
partner entering their own data and invited users returning to add more of their
financial picture are the next signals.

## Consequences

Each cohort tests a concrete expansion in the product model and interface before
public acquisition adds support load. The project can learn from financial data
that users have chosen to maintain, not from stated interest alone.

Progress will look slower than a launch measured by sign-ups. Cohort evidence can
also overrepresent people close to the founder, so general-market claims must wait
for the later cohort.

## Alternatives considered

### Launch publicly after the first usable build

This could gather more feedback sooner. It was rejected because weak retention
from a broad audience would not explain whether the problem was the core workflow,
missing household support, or premature acquisition.

### Optimize for daily or weekly active users

This was rejected because the product should reduce financial administration.
Frequent visits can indicate extra work rather than better outcomes.
