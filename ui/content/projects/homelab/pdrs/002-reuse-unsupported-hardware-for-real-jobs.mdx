## Context

The lab began with several working devices that their vendors no longer
supported well: a 2011 printer, a Pascal-generation GPU, and older phones.
Replacing them would spend money and discard useful hardware when software was
the immediate constraint.

Reuse still needed a purpose. Running an old device merely because it could
boot would add power, space, and maintenance without improving the lab.

## Decision

Give unsupported hardware a second life when it can perform a real current job
with maintained open software. Prefer adapting software over buying a
replacement when the existing device remains capable and safe.

The printer will remain available through a network print server. The desktop
GPU may run batch computer-vision work under a pinned operating-system and
driver configuration. Old phones remain candidates for work that needs real
mobile hardware.

Do not treat reuse as a reason to invent workloads. Each device must solve an
identified problem, and the lab may retire it when the job disappears or the
maintenance cost overtakes the benefit.

## Consequences

The policy can avoid purchases and electronic waste while retaining hardware
whose distinctive capability still matters. It also accepts support work that
a new device might avoid, including old drivers, network bridges, and battery
care.

The policy was later too permissive for the noisy, idle Asus desktop. PDR 000
replaced it with a stricter rule that also accounts for placement, noise,
power, cabling, and an observed capacity need.

## Evaluation

Reuse succeeds when the device performs a named job that would otherwise need
another purchase. Revisit the choice when a device lacks a workload, depends
on unsafe software, or costs more to house and maintain than to replace.
