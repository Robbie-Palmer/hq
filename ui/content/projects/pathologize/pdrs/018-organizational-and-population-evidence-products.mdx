## Context

Pathology data products usually start with a patient, slide, tissue structure, or cell. The same evidence
also supports questions about a cohort, laboratory, institution, health system, or population. Those
questions are not merely dashboard slices. They can reveal sampling bias, scanner and preparation shift,
data-quality failures, service demand, clinical workflow effects, and epidemiological variation that no
individual record can establish.

Aggregation changes the signal. A prevalence or survival estimate describes a defined group over a
defined period. It carries less information about any one person than the records that contributed to it.
SEER makes this limit explicit for survival statistics: group results cannot predict exactly what will
happen to an individual patient. Pathologize needs the same boundary while retaining an authorized route
back to supporting evidence when an aggregate result needs investigation.

## Proposed decision

Treat cohort, institution, health-system, and population views as first-class aggregate data products.
Each product must state its question, denominator, eligibility, observation window, source coverage,
missingness, uncertainty, privacy class, and intended action. It must preserve bidirectional provenance
to the patient, slide, tissue-structure, and cell products that support it, subject to access controls.

### Aggregate product levels

| Product level | Questions it may answer                                                                                                                                                      | Required boundaries                                                                                                                                                   |
| ------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Cohort        | Who was sampled, who was excluded, how representative is the cohort, and how do prevalence, calibration, errors, and outcomes vary by subgroup?                              | Cohort purpose, source population, inclusion flow, case mix, missingness, time window, leakage groups, small-group limits, and comparison cohort                      |
| Institution   | How do one site's scanners, stains, preparation, artifacts, rejection and rescan rates, turnaround, routing, concordance, and service demand affect quality and performance? | Site and laboratory identity, workflow version, scanner and protocol periods, local case mix, staffing context, and change points                                     |
| Health system | How do sites differ in capacity, referrals, screening yield, biomarker prevalence, treatment, outcomes, access, and operational impact?                                      | Comparable definitions across sites, referral and routing rules, duplicate-patient handling, privacy controls, and both site-specific and pooled estimates            |
| Population    | How do incidence, prevalence, screening yield, biomarker distribution, treatment, recurrence, survival, and outcomes vary across place, time, and demographic groups?        | Defined population and denominator, ascertainment, age or risk standardisation, coverage, censoring, reporting delay, confounding, and limits on individual inference |

An aggregate product may combine measured pathology evidence with permitted clinical, operational, treatment,
or outcome records. It must distinguish observed counts from estimates and pathology findings from later
events. A treatment or outcome association is not a treatment-effect claim unless the study design supports
that interpretation.

### Bidirectional evidence

Every aggregate result must support two directions of inquiry:

* Upward provenance identifies which eligible patients, slides, structures, and cells contributed, which
  records were missing or rejected, and how each record affected the denominator or estimate.
* Downward drill-down lets an authorized reviewer move from an aggregate change to the permitted lower-level
  evidence needed to investigate it. Public readers receive safe summaries or approved examples, not a route
  around patient or source restrictions.

The connection also runs from aggregate findings back into product work. A scanner-specific error can trigger
data-quality review. An underrepresented group can trigger source rebalancing or external validation. A rise in
rejection, rescan, turnaround, or routing delay can trigger workflow changes. A prevalence or screening-yield
gap can trigger a new lower-level enrichment product. None of those findings establishes the diagnosis,
biomarker, prognosis, or treatment response of an individual patient.

### Required analyses and limits

Cover the following when they are relevant to the product question:

* stain, preparation, scanner, compression, artifact, rejection, and rescan effects;
* site shift, case mix, referral patterns, workflow, routing, turnaround, service demand, and capacity;
* diagnostic and reader concordance, calibration, subgroup performance, and screening yield;
* biomarker prevalence, treatment variation, recurrence, survival, and other outcome variation; and
* temporal drift before and after scanner, protocol, staffing, service, or population changes.

Publish denominators and uncertainty beside rates. Report missingness by source and group. Separate a real
site effect from a scanner or preparation effect when the data permit it, and state when they remain
confounded. Do not pool away a harmful subgroup result. Suppress, combine, or restrict small groups when
disclosure risk or unstable estimates outweighs detail.

Case mix, ascertainment, treatment selection, referral, access, calendar time, and missing follow-up can all
confound aggregate comparisons. Define these factors before interpreting an institution or population
difference. Re-evaluate the product when source coverage, scanner mix, protocols, coding, treatment, or the
underlying population changes.

Dashboard layout, warehouse design, aggregation engine, storage, model, and materialization remain architecture
choices. The product contract is the aggregate question, evidence, privacy boundary, drill-down right, quality
limits, and action it supports.

## Consequences

Pathologize can treat data quality, business and clinical operations, and epidemiology as product questions in
their own right. Aggregate products can expose why a lower-level benchmark transfers poorly and whether an
apparent improvement survives another site, scanner, subgroup, or time period.

The products require linkage and governance beyond image labels. Privacy risk can increase when several rare
attributes are combined even if direct identifiers are absent. Small groups will sometimes need suppression,
and restricted reviewers may be the only people allowed to inspect the supporting records.

Aggregate results will remain weaker for individual decisions than direct patient, slide, structure, or cell
evidence. That is a useful constraint. It keeps service planning, prevalence, and population outcomes from
becoming unqualified claims about one person.

## Evaluation

Accept the proposal when one institution or cohort product traces an artifact, shift, or workflow finding down
to authorized supporting records and back up to a corrected aggregate. A separate population or health-system
product must report denominators, missingness, confounding, temporal limits, privacy controls, and subgroup
uncertainty. Reject the proposal if either product exposes restricted records, hides a harmful small-group result,
or presents an aggregate association as an individual finding.
