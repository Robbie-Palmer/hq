## Context

Pathology institutes buy a scanning service, not a box with a maximum
slides-per-hour number. A useful choice depends on the slides the institute handles,
the images the scanner produces, the work needed to keep it running, and what those
images allow people and analytical systems to do. Purchase price is only one part of
the cost. Failed scans, technician attention, service response, storage, network and
compute demand, validation, delayed cases, and an awkward exit can cost more over the
life of the system.

Vendor specifications are necessary but answer a narrow question. Leica reports a
sustained 81 slides per hour for a 15 mm by 15 mm area at native 40x on the Aperio GT
450 DX. Hamamatsu reports more than 82 slides per hour for the same nominal area on
the NanoZoomer S360MD when using five focus points. The products use different focus,
optical, compression, and output arrangements. Those are vendor claims under stated
conditions, not evidence that either scanner will deliver that rate on an institute's
slide mix.

A 2025 comparison scanned the same 347-slide set in 16 runs across eight high-volume
scanner models at one institute. Total run time ranged from about 13.5 to 47 hours,
and the stringent image review found markedly different rates and types of missing
tissue, blur, barcode, and other errors. A separate production report observed a
1.19% rescan rate in its established workflow. These findings do not conflict. The
study designs, slide mixes, review thresholds, staff experience, scan modes, and
purposes differ. Procurement intelligence must retain those conditions rather than
turn the percentages into universal product scores.

Scanner choice can reach beyond image appearance. Paired scans of the same prostate
slides on three devices produced different colour values and several different
tissue and cell-density measurements. A four-scanner study found scanner-sensitive
lymph-node segmentation. Another study rescanned 1,274 prostate slides on a second
vendor's system and observed a three percentage-point reduction in model AUC. More
recent controlled work found scanner variation to be an important source of
foundation-model performance loss. These results establish a risk worth testing.
They do not establish that every measurement, biomarker, or model will move in the
same direction on a named scanner.

Diagnostic evidence needs the same care. A vendor-sponsored, blinded, randomized
crossover study of 1,992 cases found whole-slide imaging noninferior to microscopy
across its intended case mix. Its disclosed sponsorship, paid participation, scanner
system, reader design, washout, stains, and diagnostic subgroups are part of the
claim. The result supports the tested system and use. It neither ranks scanner models
nor proves local concordance after a different firmware, viewer, workflow, or case
mix is introduced. Current CAP guidance therefore calls for local validation that
matches intended clinical use and for reconsideration after material system changes.

Specimen preparation is a separate product dimension owned by PDR 020. Fixation,
processing, sectioning, stain protocol and intensity, mounting, coverslip quality,
folds, bubbles, debris, and tissue morphology are not scanner attributes. They can
change how tissue detection, focus, colour reproduction, compression, or image review
behaves. PDR 019 records that interaction and imports preparation evidence as context
or a confounder. It must not relabel preparation-driven morphology, image artifacts,
or outcome differences as scanner effects.

Institutes therefore need an evidence product that connects a named scanner
configuration to their intended outcomes without pretending that one device wins for
all sites and uses.

## Proposed decision

Build an institute-facing scanner and image-quality procurement and partnership
intelligence product. It will turn vendor material, institute experience, controlled
comparisons, and bounded inference into decision records for purchase, validation,
upgrade, replacement, and commercial or research partnership choices.

The product will answer these questions:

* Which scanner configurations fit the institute's intended diagnostic, research,
  quantitative, and analytical uses, at its expected case, tissue, stain, and volume
  mix?
* What does each candidate claim, what has this institute or a comparable institute
  observed, what has a controlled comparison isolated, and what remains inferred or
  unknown?
* What image-quality and operational failures matter for the intended use, who
  detects them, what gets rescanned or rejected, and what happens to turnaround when
  the normal path or fallback fails?
* What local validation is needed before purchase or use, and what firmware,
  software, optical, format, compression, integration, or service change should
  trigger review or revalidation?
* Does an upgrade solve a measured constraint without creating a storage, compute,
  interoperability, validation, or supplier-exit cost that outweighs the benefit?
* Is a vendor or institute partnership filling a real evidence gap under acceptable
  data rights, publication, conflict, support, and exit terms, or merely funding a
  favourable demonstration?

### Describe the scanner that produced the evidence

Every decision record will identify the vendor, model, individual unit where known,
firmware and acquisition software version, scan mode, and evidence date. It will
describe the objective and numerical aperture or equivalent optical configuration,
native pixel spacing, illumination and sensor path, colour handling, focus method,
focus-point behaviour, tissue detection, z-plane options, automatic or manual
rescan, and the handling of failed or skipped slides.

The same record will state the container and image format, codec and compression
setting, pyramid and focal-plane behaviour, metadata and physical-scale support, and
any conversion before use. A claim of DICOM output is not enough. The relevant DICOM
conformance statement, supported transfer and workflow behaviour, and tested exchange
with the institute's systems matter. The current DICOM whole-slide microscopy model
and IHE image-acquisition profile provide standards-based comparison points, but a
standard named in a brochure is not proof of working interoperability.

Operational fit will cover slide and rack capacity, throughput under stated tissue
area and focus conditions, first-pass success, rescan and rejection behaviour,
operator hands-on time, scheduled and unscheduled downtime, recovery, consumables,
preventive maintenance, service hours, response and resolution commitments, parts
availability, remote-access terms, training, product life, and upgrade policy. It
will distinguish a contractual support promise from response and repair times that
an institute observed.

Interoperability will include patient and slide identity, barcode handling, order and
case association, laboratory and image-management integration, viewer compatibility,
standards-based import and export, access to original images and metadata, bulk
export, audit needs, cybersecurity responsibility, and migration at contract end.
Proprietary formats are not an automatic rejection, and a standards-based format is
not an automatic pass. The decision is whether the tested workflow preserves
identity, pixels, physical scale, metadata, performance, and a credible exit.

Storage and compute effects will be tied to the institute's slides. The record will
cover image size distributions rather than a single average, ingest and retrieval
bandwidth, codec support and decode cost, cache and viewer behaviour, analysis time,
retention, backup, replication, archive retrieval, conversion, and duplicate storage
during migration or validation. A smaller lossy image is not free capacity if its
compression changes a diagnostic feature or analytical result. Human-observer work
has found task performance stable at some JPEG 2000 compression levels and worse at
higher ratios in one bounded breast-biopsy experiment. Its scope is too narrow to
establish a universal safe threshold.

Total cost will include acquisition or lease, software and interface licences,
servers, network and storage, compute, backup, facility work, racks and accessories,
installation, validation, training, scanning and quality-control labour, service,
planned maintenance, downtime, rescans, glass fallback, cybersecurity work,
upgrades, migration, and disposal or exit. It will show both the cost over the chosen
term and the operational assumptions behind it. An institute cost study found that
scanner acquisition was 21% of its annualized digital-pathology costs while IT
hardware and software accounted for 33%. That observation is useful for finding
omitted costs, not for importing the same percentages into another institute.

### Follow image evidence to institute outcomes

The product will keep the chain from source condition to outcome visible:

* At acquisition, it will connect optics, focus, tissue detection, scan mode,
  compression, and format to coverage, colour, contrast, resolution, blur, stitching,
  dropout, barcode failure, file integrity, artifact burden, rescan, and slide
  rejection.
* At diagnostic use, it will connect accepted and failed images to glass fallback,
  review time, diagnostic concordance and disagreement, ancillary work, case
  turnaround, delayed or redistributed work, and the uncertainty pathologists report.
* At quantitative use, it will connect image properties to cell detection and counts,
  nuclear or cellular measurements, tissue segmentation, compartment areas and
  densities, spatial measurements, and repeatability across units and time.
* At slide and patient level, it will keep slide findings separate from case and
  patient aggregation. It will report whether scanner-related disagreement changes a
  biomarker, model output, confidence, abstention, referral, or other downstream
  finding, and whether that change survives patient-level analysis.
* At institute level, it will connect first-pass yield, technician and pathologist
  time, capacity, downtime, service, storage, compute, rework, fallback, and delayed
  cases to throughput, turnaround, continuity, staffing, cost, and the ability to
  serve intended clinical or research partnerships.

Preparation and stain quality will enter this chain as separate inputs. The product
may state that folds or a thick, pale, dark, dirty, wet, or poorly coverslipped slide
interacted with one scanner's focus or tissue detection. It may compare how scanners
handled the same prepared slide. It will assign the source of an observed difference
to preparation, scanner, their interaction, or unresolved origin. It will not claim
that the scanner caused the underlying fold, staining variation, section thickness,
cell morphology, or patient outcome.

### Keep four evidence classes separate

Every material statement will carry one of four evidence classes:

* A **vendor claim** comes from a dated specification, conformance statement,
  regulatory submission, contract, service term, or documented demonstration. The
  record preserves its test conditions, version, jurisdiction, intended use, and
  sponsor.
* An **institute observation** describes production or evaluation experience at a
  named site and period. It preserves workload, staffing, preparation and stain mix,
  quality threshold, denominator, device unit, firmware, failures, missing data, and
  relevant workflow changes.
* A **controlled comparison** holds important conditions constant. Strong scanner
  evidence uses the same glass slide across devices, repeat scans across units or
  days, or a prespecified paired design. Diagnostic comparisons use an appropriate
  reader crossover, randomized order where possible, washout, adjudication, and the
  same case information in each condition.
* An **inferred effect** projects an observation to another site, workload, outcome,
  or cost. It states the linking assumptions, uncertainty, and evidence gaps. It
  never inherits the confidence of its source observation.

The product will not average these classes into one score. A vendor claim does not
become independent evidence because several brochures repeat it. A large production
dataset can describe one institute precisely while remaining confounded. A small
paired comparison can isolate a scanner effect while representing too little case
mix to predict routine operations.

### Judge local fit and causal reach

Each conclusion will name the case, tissue, stain, specimen, preparation, and intended
use mix it covers. It will preserve variation by relevant diagnostic group, tissue,
stain, specimen size, slide density, site, scanner unit, time period, and patient
subgroup rather than hiding weak performance in an overall mean. Small subgroup
results will carry their uncertainty and will not create a winner by chance.

Time is part of the evidence. Firmware, acquisition software, calibration, service,
optical components, device age, operator experience, volume, preparation process,
viewer, network, codec, model, and downstream workflow can change. Evidence will
identify the period and configuration it describes. Repeated observations can reveal
drift, but a before-and-after change cannot assign cause when several components
changed together. One histochemical quantification study found less than 0.5%
within-batch variation on one scanner but 7% variation between scanners or over time,
which shows why a dated scanner identity belongs with the measurement.

Site and scanner effects are often entangled with preparation, case mix, staffing,
viewer, and clinical practice. Models can also learn site signatures that correlate
with outcomes or patient groups. A multisite study found that such signatures biased
predictions of clinical and genomic features and could expose ethnicity-associated
site information. The product will therefore treat an association between a scanner
and a slide or patient finding as confounded until the design separates the scanner
from site, preparation, tissue and stain mix, and outcome prevalence.

Paired scans of the same slide are preferred for scanner effects on image properties
and analytical output. Replicate units and repeat dates help separate model,
individual-device, and temporal effects. Reader crossover evidence is preferred for
diagnostic concordance. Even these designs have limits. Repeated scans do not prove a
change in patient outcome, one slide cannot represent a service, and reader washout
does not remove all memory or learning. Unpaired vendor cohorts, simple pre-post
deployments, and cross-institute comparisons support description or hypotheses, not
causal scanner claims.

### Protect institutes and patients in partnerships

The product will prefer aggregate operational evidence when patient- or slide-level
data is unnecessary. When linkage is needed to assess case or patient outcomes, the
record will state the approved purpose, lawful or ethical basis, minimum fields,
retention, access, de-identification or pseudonymisation limits, and whether a vendor
can use the data or derived results. DICOM confidentiality profiles can guide
de-identification, but applying a profile does not by itself settle re-identification,
free-text, label-image, genomic, rare-disease, or cross-dataset linkage risk.

Funding, donated equipment, paid readers, vendor employees, consultancy, publication
control, data access, intellectual property, and a partner's right to reuse images or
results will appear beside the evidence they affect. A commercial conflict does not
invalidate a study. Hiding it prevents an institute from judging the study's design,
reporting freedom, and incentive to favour one interpretation.

Recommendations will be conditional statements such as "fits this H\&E workload if
the observed first-pass yield holds and the DICOM exchange passes local validation."
They may support a purchase, a bounded validation, an upgrade, a mixed fleet, a
partnership with safeguards, further evidence, or no action. They will not produce an
unqualified vendor league table or a single rank that lets a fast scanner compensate
for missing tissue, weak service, poor interoperability, or unsupported analytical
use.

## Consequences

Institutes can trace a recommendation to their use, workload, evidence quality, and
unresolved risks. Vendors still get credit for strong specifications, service, and
evidence, but their claims remain distinct from local performance. Procurement can
consider diagnostic and analytical quality alongside capacity and cost without
pretending that these outcomes share one unit or trade against each other cleanly.

The product may conclude that the available evidence cannot distinguish two options.
That is useful when a confident rank would rest on unmatched slides, different stains,
old firmware, sponsored reporting, or another institute's workflow. It also makes
evidence gaps concrete enough to shape validation terms or a partnership agreement.

Maintaining configuration, time, intended-use, conflict, and causal context costs more
than copying specification sheets. Some service and price terms will be confidential,
some institutes will lack reliable downtime or rescan records, and some vendor formats
will make independent inspection difficult. A recommendation will expire as products,
contracts, workflows, and evidence change.

The separation from specimen preparation prevents a common attribution error, but it
also means one decision may need evidence from both product dimensions. PDR 019 can
show that a scanner is less tolerant of a preparation condition. It cannot decide how
the institute should change fixation, sectioning, staining, mounting, or other
preparation work.

## Alternatives considered

### Publish a vendor specification catalogue

This would make models, formats, and headline throughput easier to find, but it would
not answer whether a scanner works on the institute's slides, systems, staffing, or
downstream analyses. It would also give vendor claims and controlled observations an
unhelpfully similar appearance.

### Rank vendors with one weighted score

This was rejected because weights hide the intended use and let unrelated strengths
cancel serious failures. It would also collapse different firmware, service regions,
commercial terms, case mixes, and evidence quality into a false universal order.

### Compare image quality without operational or downstream outcomes

Optical test objects and same-slide comparisons are useful, but a scanner that
reproduces colour or resolution well may still fail the institute on tissue capture,
workflow, service, cost, interoperability, diagnostic use, or a named analytical task.

### Build the evaluation and analytics platform now

Running studies and choosing data, statistical, or model architecture would turn this
product decision into an implementation programme. PDR 019 defines what evidence a
decision needs and how far it can support a claim. Separate work can decide how to
produce that evidence.

## Non-goals

This proposal does not define study execution, a dashboard, a data warehouse,
statistical implementation, model architecture, scanner-control software, a universal
image-quality threshold, or preparation workflow. It does not certify a scanner for
clinical use, replace institute validation, negotiate a procurement, or claim that a
digital finding improves patient outcomes without direct evidence.

## Evaluation

Accept the proposal when one real purchase, validation, upgrade, or partnership
decision can be reviewed from the product and an institute can answer all of the
following:

* The candidate scanner, unit or model, firmware, optics, focus and rescan behaviour,
  compression, format, throughput conditions, downtime, service, interoperability,
  storage, compute, and total-cost assumptions are identifiable.
* Vendor claims, institute observations, controlled comparisons, and inferred effects
  remain separate and retain source, date, intended use, conflicts, and uncertainty.
* Evidence connects preparation and stain quality, image artifacts, rejection,
  concordance, turnaround, measurements, slide and patient findings, biomarker or
  model performance, subgroups, and operations without treating them as one score.
* Preparation-driven morphology and outcomes remain owned by the separate preparation
  dimension. Scanner interactions are visible, and unsupported scanner attribution is
  rejected.
* The recommendation reflects local case, tissue, stain, workload, and service needs,
  states causal limits, and can support a conditional choice without publishing a
  simplistic vendor league table.

Reject the proposal if the product mainly republishes brochures, ranks vendors from
unmatched evidence, treats association as scanner causation, hides commercial or
privacy terms, or requires a dashboard, warehouse, study runner, statistical method,
or model design before it can explain a decision.
