## Context

A tissue specimen is finite. Choosing how to preserve, process, embed, section, and stain it allocates that
material between immediate morphology, later diagnostic work, and possible molecular or research assays. The
choice also commits staff time, equipment, consumables, storage capacity, safety controls, and money. A fast
answer can consume tissue needed for a later test. A preparation that gives crisp morphology can alter nucleic
acids, antigens, proteins, metabolites, or cell measurements. A method that preserves one analyte can make
routine sectioning or margin assessment harder.

These effects are specific to the tissue, question, protocol, and endpoint. Frozen-section concordance varies
by organ and diagnostic problem, and errors can arise from sampling, technical artifacts, or interpretation.
Cold ischaemia can change gene and protein expression before a sample reaches its preservative. Fixation type
and duration can change morphology, immunoreactivity, in situ hybridisation, and DNA or RNA quality. A paired
study of fresh, frozen, and FFPE cardiac tissue found degraded FFPE RNA and preparation-dependent transcript
profiles, yet high correlation for protein-coding transcripts. That result is useful for its studied endpoint.
It does not make frozen tissue or FFPE universally better.

Current standards already treat preparation as evidence that travels with a result. The 2025
[CAP specimen-handling guide](https://www.cap.org/wp-content/uploads/documents/Practical_Guide_to_Specimen_Handling_in_Surgical_Pathology_2025.pdf?download=true)
connects collection and handling to tissue-specific requirements. CAP's 2025
[quantitative IHC protocol](https://www.cap.org/wp-content/uploads/protocols/IHC.Bmk_1.2.0.0.REL_CAPCP.pdf?download=true)
records cold ischaemia, fixative, decalcification, and fixation time beside biomarker findings. The
[NCI snap-freezing practice](https://biospecimens.cancer.gov/bestpractices/Appendix5.pdf) links freezing
conditions to morphology, DNA, RNA, and protein uses while noting that some media suit only some assays.

Institutes still have to turn broad standards and published comparisons into local SOPs. Their case mix,
transport times, equipment, assays, expertise, and acceptable turnaround differ. Evidence produced at another
institute rarely reproduces the same conditions. A static list of preferred methods would hide those
limits and would become stale as assays, standards, staff, reagents, and case mix change.

## Proposed decision

Build an institute-facing specimen preparation and SOP intelligence product. It will help an institute choose
and govern a locally approved preparation path for a stated tissue, question, time constraint, and downstream
test plan. It will explain why a path is eligible, what it may change, what local validation supports it, and
when a person must review an exception or unresolved risk.

The product will not direct patient care. Pathologists, laboratory leaders, safety specialists, and other
authorised institute experts remain responsible for clinical and laboratory policy. The product will not call
one preparation method best in the abstract.

### Questions the product answers

For clinical work, the product answers which locally validated preparation paths can support the stated
diagnostic question or margin assessment in the available time. It shows expected morphology and artifacts,
the basis and limits of diagnostic confidence, known frozen-to-permanent or other method concordance, and the
conditions that require deferral, repeat work, another specimen, or expert escalation. It keeps a slide finding
separate from the patient-level finding to which an authorised expert relates it.

For laboratory work, it answers how cold ischaemia, fixation, decalcification, embedding, sectioning, and
staining may affect morphology, artifacts, tissue consumption, repeat work, and suitability for IHC, ISH, DNA,
RNA, protein, metabolite, spatial, cell-based, or other assays. It shows whether a result depends on a locally
validated combination rather than treating each preparation step as interchangeable.

For research, it answers whether specimens prepared under different SOPs remain suitable for a proposed
measurement or comparison. It exposes preparation-related confounding, the relevance of paired evidence, and
the limits of transferring a finding across tissues, assays, periods, or institutes. Research suitability does
not imply clinical validity.

For operations, it answers what each eligible path demands in turnaround, scheduled and on-call staffing,
skills, equipment access, consumables, required preservation or storage conditions, safety controls, repeat and
rework risk, and total cost. Total cost includes tissue lost to failed or duplicate work, staff and specialist
time, consumables, equipment use, safety obligations, storage burden, validation, delays, and downstream work
made unusable. The first stain or block accounts for only part of it.

### Account for the complete preparation path

Represent an SOP as a linked preparation path rather than a label such as "frozen" or "FFPE". The path starts
with the specimen's fresh state and elapsed warm and cold ischaemia. It then identifies preservation or
fixation type and duration, any decalcification, embedding medium and orientation, sectioning policy, stains and
their order, and the material left for later work. It also states which assays and findings local validation
covers.

The product must cover these preparation families without treating a family as one uniform method:

| Preparation family                            | Questions it may support                                                                                                         | Effects and trade-offs to expose                                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| --------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Fresh tissue                                  | Rapid gross or cytological assessment, viable-cell or tissue work, and allocation before preservation                            | Continuing ischaemia and autolysis, infection and handling risk, immediate staffing, rapid allocation pressure, and loss of later options when scarce tissue is consumed                                                                                     |
| Intraoperative frozen section                 | Time-critical morphology, adequacy, tissue identification, and margin assessment for locally validated indications               | Ice crystal, tearing, folding, compression, freezing and sampling artifacts, tissue-dependent concordance and deferral, cryostat staffing and equipment, contamination controls, tissue consumed, and correlation with later permanent sections              |
| Fresh-frozen material                         | DNA, RNA, protein, metabolite, and other assays validated for frozen material, with cryosection morphology where suitable        | Freezing rate, ice damage, OCT or other medium compatibility, cold-chain and freeze-thaw exposure, sectioning difficulty, equipment, energy and storage burden, and reduced suitability for some routine morphology or stains                                |
| FFPE                                          | Durable morphology, routine H\&E and special stains, and locally validated IHC, ISH, DNA, RNA, protein, spatial, or other assays | Cold ischaemia, formalin chemistry and exposure, fixation depth and duration, processing heat and solvents, cross-linking and fragmentation, paraffin and sectioning artifacts, longer turnaround, analyte-specific recovery, and block or slide consumption |
| Other stabilised, fixed, or embedded variants | A defined compromise where an institute has evidence for the tissue and intended endpoints                                       | Method-specific morphology and analyte effects, new equipment or consumables, safety and storage conditions, compatibility with existing assays, and the cost of separate validation and staff competence                                                    |

The final family includes alcohol-based or molecular fixatives, PAXgene-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue,
RNAlater or related stabilisation, OCT and other cryomedia, cryopreservation, resin embedding, and methods for
specific fresh or calcified tissues when relevant. These are not drop-in equivalents. For example, a
[comparative PAXgene study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27402607/) found endpoint-dependent differences
across morphology, IHC, DNA, RNA, and proteomics and required protocol optimisation. The product will preserve
that conditional account instead of turning the study into a general preference.

### Relate published datasets without rewriting them

Apply the source-native boundary from
[PDR 009](/projects/pathologize/pdrs/009-source-native-pathology-data-mesh) when third-party datasets contribute
preparation evidence. Keep each published dataset as one source product even when it mixes FFPE, frozen-section,
fresh-frozen, fresh, or other material. Preserve the publisher's preparation facts on each native specimen or
slide, including an explicit unknown when the source does not report them. Do not split a source into artificial
preparation-specific datasets or assign one dataset-wide method that erases mixed preparation.

Downstream SOP and preparation-comparison products select and combine the relevant native records while
retaining their source identity. They refer to the source images rather than copying them into a new product.
A published dataset establishes availability alone. Preparation suitability for another institute or intended
use requires separate evidence. Dataset discovery belongs to the separate source catalog outcome. This decision
does not define catalog, storage, or dataset-composition mechanics.

### Connect each preparation step to outcomes

Cold ischaemia and fixation must remain visible as separate variables. Record the measured or bounded delay,
conditions before preservation, fixative chemistry, specimen dimensions relevant to penetration, and fixation
duration. Connect them to morphology, autolysis, antigenicity, IHC and ISH signal, DNA and RNA integrity,
protein and phosphoprotein measurements, and other validated endpoints. Published observations that expression
can change within minutes after excision justify tracking the interval. They do not establish one safe interval
for every tissue and assay. Likewise, a fixation bound from a breast biomarker standard cannot silently become
policy for another organ or analyte.

For decalcification, identify the agent, duration, sequence relative to fixation, and intended downstream work.
Show the trade between turnaround, morphology, antigen preservation, ISH, and nucleic-acid recovery. One
[prospective comparison](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32094425/) found false-negative IHC and molecular
results after hydrochloric acid or long formic-acid protocols, while EDTA and short formic-acid protocols
preserved more of the studied endpoints. That evidence informs a choice for similar material. It does not prove
that one agent is always superior, because specimen size, mineralisation, endpoint, and acceptable delay differ.

For embedding, account for medium, orientation, heat and processing exposure, edge and margin representation,
sectioning feasibility, morphology, assay compatibility, equipment, consumables, and time. Paraffin, OCT,
resin, and other media solve different problems. The product will show when an orientation or embedding choice
changes which surface or compartment a reviewer can assess. Treat embedding as part of the evidence because it
changes the material under review.

For sectioning, account for thickness, levels, serial order, surface depletion, curls, folds, chatter,
compression, contamination, and the number of sections reserved or consumed. Section thickness can alter IHC
intensity and the apparent proportion of positive cells, as shown in a
[controlled thickness comparison](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29084061/). It also changes cell and tissue
measurements through overlap, partial profiles, lost small objects, and altered apparent area or density. The
product must relate any measurement to the preparation and section sampled. It must not imply that a slide-level
count is automatically a patient-level quantity.

For staining, identify the stain family, counterstain, pretreatment, sequence, and prior use of the section when
these facts affect interpretation or later assays. Connect staining to contrast, artifacts, fading or masking,
antigen or nucleic-acid accessibility, destaining and reuse limits, cell and tissue measurements, slide findings,
repeat work, and remaining material. H\&E, special stains, IHC, ISH, and stains used before dissection or spatial
assays can make different demands on the same section. The product describes those dependencies without
choosing a staining platform or laboratory protocol implementation.

### Keep four kinds of knowledge distinct

Every claim or policy presented by the product must carry one of four origins:

1. Published evidence identifies the standard or study, its version, tissue, cohort, preparation details,
   endpoint, comparator, and institute context. It records whether the evidence is paired and whether the paper
   studied diagnostic concordance, analytical suitability, morphology, resources, or another outcome.
2. Local observations describe what the institute saw under a named SOP version and period. They retain case
   mix, operators or services, assay and equipment context, exceptions, and missing data. An observation is not
   automatically causal or transferable.
3. Expert policy records an authorised institute decision, its owner, rationale, scope, review date, and any
   permitted exception. Policy may be more conservative than published evidence and must not masquerade as a
   research result.
4. Inferred effects state the assumptions and reasoning that connect known preparation effects to an outcome
   without direct evidence. They carry explicit uncertainty and cannot become local fact through repetition.

A comparison or recommendation may draw on all four, but it must show the contribution and conflicts from each
class. Unknown preparation details remain unknown. The product must not fill them with a typical SOP or infer
that an old block followed the current method.

### Bound applicability and uncertainty

Published and local comparisons must expose case mix and pre-analytic confounding. Relevant differences include
tissue and disease composition, specimen type and size, cellularity, tumour fraction, necrosis, calcification,
warm and cold ischaemia, transport, prior treatment, fixation penetration and duration, processor and reagent
conditions, embedding and sectioning, stain and assay versions, operators, equipment, storage age, and the
amount and region sampled. These variables can alter morphology, assay success, findings, turnaround, and cost.

Give paired evidence particular weight because two preparations from the same patient or specimen reduce some
between-case variation. Still record whether the comparison used adjacent pieces, a split specimen, serial
sections, or repeat measures. Tissue heterogeneity, destructive sectioning, surface depletion, and unequal
aliquots mean paired samples are not identical. Unpaired evidence can remain useful when its case mix and limits
are clear.

Attach evidence and local observations to time-bounded SOP, assay, reagent, equipment, and policy versions.
Flag temporal drift after staff changes, new instruments, reagent or stain changes, revised standards, altered
case mix, longer storage, or a shift in downstream testing. A historical concordance or failure rate cannot
silently describe current practice.

Rare tissues, unusual combinations, damaged specimens, urgent out-of-hours cases, and new downstream assays
will often lack close evidence. The product will show the nearest evidence, why it may not transfer, and what
remains uncertain. It will favour escalation over a precise-looking estimate built from unlike cases.

Evidence from another institute starts as published or external evidence, not local validation. Differences in
grossing, transport, staffing, processors, assays, interpretation, safety rules, cost base, and case mix can
change both outcome and feasibility. Transfer requires an explicit applicability judgment and local validation
at the level required by institute policy and the intended use.

### Support selection, exceptions, escalation, validation, and review

For a selection, the institute states the tissue and specimen type, clinical or research question, available
time, amount and quality of material, planned downstream tests, and material operating constraints. The product
returns the locally eligible SOPs and compares their expected consequences. It may explain that two paths serve
different priorities. It must not hide alternatives behind a single score.

An exception records the unmet SOP condition, reason, available material, expected effects, tests or findings
at risk, uncertainty, tissue-conservation consequence, and the expert policy that permits or rejects the
departure. Institute policy requires escalation when the intended use lacks local validation, evidence conflicts,
a rare scenario exceeds policy, a preparation failure could exhaust irreplaceable tissue, a rapid result has
material discordance risk, or a safety or resource constraint cannot be met.

For validation, the product states the claim that needs support and the required evidence boundary. It can call
for relevant tissues, paired material where feasible, intended assays, morphology and artifact review,
concordance or repeat-work outcomes, tissue consumption, turnaround, resources, safety, and cost. It records
whether local evidence supports the proposed scope and what remains unvalidated. Study design, execution,
statistical methods, and laboratory implementation remain outside this decision.

Changed standards, material new comparative evidence, local drift, recurring exceptions or rework, assay or
equipment changes, safety findings, or changed costs and capacity trigger review. Review can retain, narrow,
expand, or retire an SOP's eligible uses. It preserves the old policy and its evidence so reviewers can
interpret earlier findings under the preparation in force at the time.

## Consequences

Institutes gain one accountable explanation for why a preparation path fits a tissue, question, deadline, and
test plan. Pathologists can see how rapid morphology and margin assessment relate to later permanent findings.
Histology, molecular, research, safety, and operations teams can discuss tissue consumption, assay suitability,
turnaround, staffing, equipment, consumables, preservation burden, and total cost without collapsing their
concerns into one quality score.

The product will also make uncertainty more visible. Some common practices will have weak paired evidence or
little local validation. A method with strong published results may remain ineligible locally. Rare scenarios
will still require judgment. Maintaining evidence applicability, SOP versions, exceptions, and review dates
adds work, and comparisons may expose disagreements that a static manual leaves implicit.

This decision does not define a study-execution system, workflow engine, dashboard, statistical method,
laboratory implementation, storage architecture or retention policy, or model architecture. It may represent
storage conditions, burden, and cost when they affect feasibility, but it does not choose freezers, archives,
repositories, or retention periods. It does not select instruments, vendors, fixatives, stains, assay platforms,
or staffing models for an institute.

## Evaluation

Accept the proposal when institute reviewers can use one product account to assess four representative
decisions:

1. a fresh specimen with a time-critical question and possible intraoperative frozen-section or margin work;
2. a small specimen that must support morphology, IHC or ISH, and planned DNA, RNA, or protein testing;
3. calcified tissue where decalcification time competes with morphology and downstream assay suitability; and
4. a research collection considering fresh-frozen, FFPE, and another preservation variant across more than one
   tissue and downstream endpoint.

For each decision, the account must identify locally eligible SOPs, show tissue use and remaining options,
connect the preparation path to morphology, artifacts, confidence and concordance, margin relevance, repeat and
rework, assay suitability, measurements, slide and patient findings, turnaround, staffing, equipment,
consumables, preservation or storage burden, safety, and total cost. It must separate published evidence, local
observations, expert policy, and inferred effects; state confounders and uncertainty; recognise paired evidence
without overstating it; expose temporal drift and rare-case limits; and explain why evidence from another
institute does or does not transfer.

Reject the proposal if it produces a universal method ranking, hides unsupported inferences inside policy,
turns slide findings into patient conclusions, prescribes care, or requires any excluded implementation or
architecture choice before an institute can make and review an evidence-informed SOP decision.
